# Dated public proof outflanks incumbent investor databases

Public data can support a database of active private-market investors in which every record carries type, sector, deals, ticket, capital and a dated proof, but only for institutional fund managers; for family offices and angels it fills part of the record at best. The US is the only jurisdiction where capital is a public field: SEC Form ADV Schedule D reports fund type and gross asset value fund by fund, and Form D dates every fundraising. Everywhere else the record must be assembled from registries (Companies House, CC0-licensed GLEIF, ESMA exports, national regulators), LP disclosures and deal announcements, and only deal announcements give broad public evidence that a firm actually deploys capital. Ticket size is disclosed nowhere and must be derived from observed deals. The incumbents do not close these gaps. PitchBook (423K–667K "investors", depending on source), Crunchbase (~300K) and Dealroom (~250K) count every name on a cap table, Preqin limits its 16,638 PE investors to confirmed allocators, prices run from about $588 to over $120K a year, every licence forbids redistribution or competing use, and no vendor publishes field-level accuracy or a per-field source and date. Measured by activity, the target universe is a few thousand firms per type (2,984 US VC firms, about 8,030 single-family offices and 3,068 corporate investors worldwide), alongside 445,535 US angels and 47,610 networked European angels. About 18% of "existing" US VC firms are zombies, and active-investor counts have fallen about 15% a year since 2021. Published benchmarks cover deals only, with curated databases missing about 15% of rounds and Crunchbase and Dealroom sharing only about 30% of entities, so ±10–15% is the realistic floor for coverage estimates. Czechia should host the VC pilot, since 30 Czech-based funds raised capital in 2011–2025 against 8 Slovak ones and Czech companies received €275m of PE/VC in 2025 against Slovakia's €7m. Slovakia's few active VCs are best compiled by hand as a gold standard. All figures are as of 25 October 2026.

## Counting only active investors shrinks the universe to a few thousand firms per type

Vendor "investor" totals count every name that appears on a cap table, so they cannot serve as a population baseline. The cleanest population series is PitchBook data published by the NVCA. It shows **2,984 US VC firms "in existence" in 2025**, meaning they raised a fund within the last eight vintage years. That is down from 3,152 in 2024 and a 3,199 peak in 2023, with **$1,376.99bn under management** ([NVCA 2026 Yearbook](https://nvca.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/NVCA-2026-Yearbook-4.9.26.pdf)).

Counts based on activity move much faster. In 2025, **13,634 investors** headquartered anywhere made at least one VC-type investment in a US company. **6,292** of them were VC, CVC or not-for-profit VC investors. The comparable 2021 figure was 25,969 active investors. That is a 47.5% fall, or roughly **15% net attrition per year** across the down-cycle ([NVCA 2026 Yearbook](https://nvca.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/NVCA-2026-Yearbook-4.9.26.pdf)).

PitchBook also counted **574 US "zombie" VC firms** at the start of 2025, up about 50% from 382 at end-2021. A zombie is a firm that raised a fund in the prior six years but has no known investment since late December 2023 ([Fortune](https://fortune.com/2025/06/02/ai-venture-capital-zombies-fundraising-mergers-ipos-bain-capital)). Against 3,152 firms in existence, an existence-based rule overstates active US VC firms by about **18%**.

The database should therefore define "active" the way the market's cleanest sources do. The rule is at least one dated, citable investment within the last 24 months: a deal announcement, a portfolio addition or a Form D for a new vehicle. A separate flag should record whether the firm raised a fund within the last six to eight years. This mirrors PitchBook's existence and zombie tests and Dealroom's practice of ranking investors by rounds in the trailing 12 months ([Dealroom methodology](https://dealroom.co/blog/the-top-100-most-prominent-vc-investors-in-europe)). With attrition at about 15% a year, activity status needs re-checking at least annually.

No primary, dated global count of PE firms is publicly available. The best regulatory anchor is SEC Form PF, which shows **2,047 advisers to 27,682 PE funds** at Q4 2025, plus 586 large "Section 4" PE advisers ([SEC Form PF](https://www.sec.gov/files/2026-08/Number_of_Funds_and_Advisers_08-05-2026a.json)). That is about 13.5 funds per adviser, so any source that lists vehicles will inflate counts more than tenfold unless records are deduplicated to the manager. The frequently quoted figure of roughly 7,000 PE firms worldwide comes from the American Investment Council, but it is undated and secondhand ([Vault](https://legacy.vault.com/industries-professions/industries/private-equity/the-industry-today)).

Family offices show the widest gap between estimate and coverage. Deloitte's model, built on Altrata/Wealth-X data, puts single-family offices at **8,030 worldwide** in 2024, up from 6,130 in 2019. It counts 3,180 in North America and projects 10,720 by 2030 ([Dakota/Deloitte](https://www.dakota.com/fundraising-news/deloitte-number-of-family-offices-to-grow-75-by-2030)). Their roughly $3.1tn in assets implies about $390m per office ([NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/business/personal-finance/family-offices-are-surpass-hedge-funds-5point4-trillion-assets-2030-rcna169812)).

Commercial trackers see far fewer. FINTRX's April 2024 breakdown listed about 1,870 single-family offices among roughly 4,000 offices in total ([FINTRX](https://www.fintrx.com/blog/a-deep-dive-into-the-fintrx-family-office-dataset)). Preqin's 2023 figure was 4,592, as cited by a competitor ([Altss](https://altss.com/vs/preqin)). The best-documented dedicated vendor therefore sees about **23% of Deloitte's single-family-office estimate**. FINTRX adds that most newly added multi-family offices are international firms not registered with the SEC, which regulatory filings alone miss ([FINTRX Q1 2026](https://www.fintrx.com/hubfs/FINTRX%20Q1%202026%20Family%20Office%20Intelligence%20Report.pdf)).

Angels are measured even less directly. UNH CVR estimates that **445,535 active US angels** invested $17.9bn in 55,346 ventures in 2024, about $40k per angel per year ([UNH CVR](https://paulcollege.unh.edu/sites/default/files/media/2025-12/FY%202024%20Analysis%20Report%20Final.pdf)). Europe has no comparable census. EBAN counts **47,610 active angels who belong to networks** across 39 countries, with €1,220m of visible investment in 4,582 rounds in 2024, or about €266k per round. EBAN has dropped its long-standing 10x multiplier for the invisible market until new research is done ([EBAN](https://www.eban.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/12/Stats-Compendium-2024.pdf)). The two bodies even disagree on the size of the US market: EBAN quotes $27.8bn against UNH CVR's $17.9bn.

Corporate investors are better bounded, with a record **3,068 corporations** investing in startups in 2025 ([GCV](https://globalventuring.com/intelligence/2026-wocv-1-contents)). No source measures how many of the world's roughly 557K UHNW individuals invest directly in private companies.

These figures show visibility falling steeply from institutions to individuals. A later plan should adopt the gradient below as a starting assumption and replace it with pilot measurements.

## US filings make AUM public, while elsewhere it must be assembled

### SEC filings give the US a regulatory backbone no other market has

SEC Form ADV is the most valuable free source. Schedule D section 7.B.(1) requires one record per private fund, giving the fund type (including "venture capital" and "private equity"), the **current gross asset value**, the minimum LP commitment, the approximate number of beneficial owners and the fund's Form D file number, which links it to fundraising filings ([Form ADV Part 1A](https://www.sec.gov/files/formadv-part1a_1.pdf)). Both SEC-registered advisers and exempt reporting advisers (ERAs) file it. Under Item 2.B an ERA can claim exemption as an adviser solely to venture capital funds under rule 203(l)-1, which is a near-definitive VC flag. ERAs do not file Item 5, so their AUM has to be summed from fund-level gross asset values. Those values are annual and gross, and exclude uncalled commitments.

The adviser population is large. A trade-association summary, not independently verified, reports 5,390 ERAs filing with the SEC and about 3,920 with states for 2023, together managing over $6tn of private-fund assets. For 2025, 16,544 SEC-registered advisers reported ([IAA](https://www.investmentadviser.org/iaatoday/news/industry-snapshot-continued-growth-in-demand-for-adviser-services); [IAA 2026](https://www.investmentadviser.org/iaatoday/press-release/2026-investment-adviser-industry-snapshot-shows-continued-growth-in-demand-for-adviser-services/)).

One engineering risk stands out. The SEC's bulk Form ADV Part 1 CSVs end on 31 December 2024. Later data is only "available" through the IAPD site, in a format this research could not confirm ([SEC FOIA Form ADV Data](https://www.sec.gov/foia-services/frequently-requested-documents/form-adv-data)). The monthly adviser reports, the latest dated October 2026, carry only a subset of items ([SEC RIA/ERA reports](https://www.sec.gov/data-research/sec-markets-data/information-about-registered-investment-advisers-exempt-reporting-advisers)).

Form PF is far weaker as a backbone. It is published only in aggregate and covers only registered advisers with at least $150m of private-fund assets ([SEC Private Fund Statistics](https://sec.gov/data-research/statistics-data-visualizations/private-fund-statistics)). It lists just **362 VC advisers** at Q4 2025 against 2,984 US VC firms, roughly 12% of US VC managers.

Form D proves that money was raised, not who deployed it. The SEC's quarterly Form D data sets (latest 2026 Q3) contain six tab-delimited tables. Their offering fields cover investment-fund type, total offering amount, total amount sold, minimum investment accepted, number of investors and sale date ([SEC Form D documentation](https://www.sec.gov/files/Form_D.pdf); [SEC Form D Data Sets](https://www.sec.gov/data-research/sec-markets-data/form-d-data-sets)). In 2025, **34,553 new Reg D offerings** raised $2,391.5bn, of which pooled funds took $2,118.3bn ([Angel Investors Network, citing DERA](https://angelinvestorsnetwork.com/market-analysis/sec-form-d-private-placements-2026-data)).

For a fund, the target size and amount sold work as a proxy for fund size, and the filing date proves fundraising. The data has three limits: the minimum investment is the LP minimum rather than the fund's ticket, an operating company's Form D lists officers, directors and promoters rather than investors, and vehicles multiply, with one tracker logging 277 new fund vehicles from 196 sponsors in September 2026 alone ([VC Beast](https://vcbeast.com/new-vc-funds-filed)).

Published counts of fund filings conflict. 100,553 pooled-fund offerings over 2009–2023 averages about 6,700 a year ([SEC-hosted presentation](https://www.sec.gov/files/craig-mccann-presentation-092123.pdf)). Yet pooled funds reportedly make up "a little less than half" of roughly 34.5k annual offerings, which would imply 15–17k a year ([freewritings.law](https://www.freewritings.law/2026/01/staff-report-from-the-office-of-the-advocate-for-small-business-capital-formation/)). The fund-only count must be recomputed from the raw tables.

EDGAR is free, but it is capped at 10 requests per second with a declared User-Agent, and full-text search is a web interface rather than a bulk source ([SEC Accessing EDGAR Data](https://www.sec.gov/search-filings/edgar-search-assistance/accessing-edgar-data); [EDGAR FTS FAQ](https://www.sec.gov/edgar/search/efts-faq.html)). 13F covers only listed holdings, and Form C crowdfunding data identifies issuers and portals, not investors ([SEC 13F](https://www.sec.gov/data-research/sec-markets-data/form-13f-data-sets); [SEC Crowdfunding](https://www.sec.gov/data-research/sec-markets-data/crowdfunding-offerings-data-sets)).

### UK and EU registries prove identity and status, not activity

The UK has the best free registry. Companies House publishes a monthly snapshot of every live company (471 MB as of 1 October 2026), a daily snapshot of persons with significant control (PSC) of about 2.4 GB, and an API that allows 600 requests per five minutes ([Companies House](https://download.companieshouse.gov.uk/en_output.html); [PSC snapshot](https://download.companieshouse.gov.uk/en_pscdata.html); [API limits](https://developer-specs.company-information.service.gov.uk/guides/rateLimiting)).

SIC 64303 ("venture and development capital") returns about **4,377 active companies** ([GB Companies directory](https://gbcomp.p-o.co.uk/en/siccode/64303)). Most are SPVs and personal vehicles, so the code generates candidates rather than proof. PSC data surfaces only stakes above 25%, which misses most minority VC holdings.

Share-allotment (SH01) filings are more powerful. Beauhurst built its product on them, and the ONS confirms they capture all filed UK unlisted equity issuance since 2015 ([ONS](https://www.ons.gov.uk/economy/nationalaccounts/uksectoraccounts/articles/economicstatisticstransformationprogramme/enhancedfinancialaccountsukflowoffundsusingbeauhurstdatatobetterunderstandtheeconomy)). Beauhurst puts unannounced rounds at 50–70% of UK activity, though its own pages disagree on the figure ([Beauhurst](https://beauhurst.com/blog/why-investment-data-varies-beauhurst-pitchbook-dealroom/); [Beauhurst vs Dealroom](https://www.beauhurst.com/lp/beauhurst-vs-dealroom/)).

The FCA Register API confirms regulated status (full-scope AIFM, small registered AIFM, RVECA) at about 50 requests per 10 seconds, but it has no bulk export ([FCA API docs](https://financial-services-register-api.readthedocs.io/en/latest/sources/financial-services-register-api.html)). The FCA's small-AIFM list also includes lapsed firms ([FCA](https://www.fca.org.uk/firms/aifmd/apply-registered-aifm)).

At EU level, ESMA's registers export authorised AIFMs and EuVECA, EuSEF and crowdfunding entities as CSV or PDF, with no documented API ([ESMA Registers](https://registers.esma.europa.eu/publication/helpPage)). Sub-threshold managers, which include most small VC houses, are registered only nationally, so no EU-level source gives manager AUM.

GLEIF's Golden Copy is the best identity spine. It is published under CC0 three times a day and includes the relationships IS_FUND-MANAGED_BY, IS_SUBFUND_OF and IS_FEEDER_TO, which map fund vehicles to their managers ([GLEIF Golden Copy](https://www.gleif.org/en/lei-data/gleif-golden-copy); [GLEIF relationship format](https://www.gleif.org/en/about-lei/common-data-file-format/relationship-record-cdf-format); [GLEIF terms](https://www.gleif.org/en/meta/lei-data-terms-of-use)).

Beneficial-ownership registers should stay out of the plan. The CJEU's November 2022 ruling ended public access. AMLD6 Article 74 now restricts access to parties who can show a legitimate interest, and Italy's register remains suspended ([McCann FitzGerald](https://www.mccannfitzgerald.com/knowledge/asset-management-and-investment-funds/public-access-to-beneficial-ownership-information-restricted); [Mondaq](https://www.mondaq.com/corporate-and-company-law/1651480/amendments-to-beneficial-ownership-regulations-following-transposition-of-eu-aml-directive); [A&O Shearman](https://www.aoshearman.com/en/insights/beneficial-ownership-register-new-access-rules-enter-into-force)).

### LP disclosures and the open web fill proof, sector and ticket

LP disclosures are the best proof that a GP really raised a fund. CalPERS publishes fund name, vintage, commitment, cash flows and net IRR for every active PE partnership as of 31 December 2025, although its web app was down when fetched on 25 October 2026 ([CalPERS](https://www.calpers.ca.gov/investments/about-investment-office/investment-organization/pep-fund-performance)). WSIB committee materials name new commitments, such as $200m to Insight Partners XI ([WSIB](https://sib.wa.gov/docs/meetings/private/25_1106content.pdf)).

European public LPs mostly publish aggregates and press releases for individual deals. The EIF made 196 equity transactions committing €7.2bn in 2024 ([EIF](https://www.eif.org/news_centre/publications/eif-annual-report-2024.htm)). Its fund-list PDFs, however, date from 30 September 2022, and the page returned a 404 ([EIF](https://eif.org/what_we_do/equity/deals/index_.htm)). KfW Capital reports €2.9bn across 153 VC funds at end-2025 but publishes no full list ([KfW](https://www.kfw.de/About-KfW/Newsroom/Latest-News/Pressemitteilungen-Details_882944.html)). LP data lacks fund size, so it has to be joined to Form D and ADV records by normalised fund name.

The open web supplies sector and ticket but carries licence risk. Wikidata is CC0 and clean but tiny: its "investor" property has 7,190 statements covering only 980 distinct investors, and only 348 items are typed directly as VC firms ([Wikidata Query Service](https://query.wikidata.org/)). OpenCorporates is ODbL share-alike, and financial institutions and corporations do not qualify as free users ([OpenCorporates](https://opencorporates.com/terms-of-use-2/)). Crunchbase's terms (30 September 2026) forbid scraping, storing significant portions and AI training, and its free Kaggle mirrors are frozen at December 2013 ([Crunchbase ToS](https://about.crunchbase.com/terms-of-service/); [Kaggle mirror](https://baselight.app/u/kaggle/dataset/justinas_startup_investments)). News wires are restrictive, with Business Wire limiting use to reading releases and RSS ([Business Wire](https://www.businesswire.com/terms-of-use)), whereas GDELT is reportedly free for commercial use with attribution ([jwatte.com](https://jwatte.com/blog/gdelt-open-data-kalev-leetaru.md)), which makes it the natural discovery index linking out to original articles. Firm websites remain the only broad source of stated ticket, sector and stage, yet practitioners advise deriving check size from recent deals because stated theses drift ([Metal](https://www.metal.so/guides/how-to-analyze-a-vc-s-investment-thesis-portfolio-and-check-sizes-in-2026/)).

Individual angels are the hardest tier, legally as well as practically. EBAN and ACA publish directories of networks, but ACA withholds individual members for privacy ([ACA](https://angelcapitalassociation.org/directory/); [EBAN](https://eban.org/membership-directory)); AngelList scraping is commonly blocked ([Coresignal](https://coresignal.com/data-sources/angellist)); LinkedIn's User Agreement of 3 November 2025 bans scraping and redistribution ([LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/legal/user-agreement)); and the Dutch data protection authority treats scraping personal data as almost always a GDPR violation while reading legitimate interest narrowly ([Pinsent Masons](https://www.pinsentmasons.com/out-law/news/dutch-web-scraping-guidance-warn-businesses-gdpr-breach-risk)).

The defensible model records networks and investing vehicles as entities. It admits an individual only with an independent dated proof, stores only professional-capacity fields, and runs an Article 14 notice process.

## Czechia and Slovakia offer open registers but thin, paywalled deal evidence

Both markets are small, and Slovakia is tiny. Invest Europe's CEE statistics show **30 Czech-based funds** raising €1,958.2m over 2011–2025, against **8 Slovak-based funds** raising €252.8m. In 2025 Czech companies received about **€275.3m** of PE/VC (0.080% of GDP). Slovak companies received about **€7m across five companies** (0.005% of GDP, the lowest in Europe) ([Invest Europe CEE 2025](https://www.investeurope.eu/media/z3pasguu/20260623_invest-europe_cee-report-2025.pdf); [Startitup](https://www.startitup.sk/slovensko-ziskalo-7-cesko-az-275-milionov-eur-nedokazeme-ziskat-zahranicny-kapital-v-europe-sme-uplne-posledni/)).

These statistics come from the EDC survey under the "market approach", which assigns investment by the portfolio company's country, and they badly under-count venture. EDC shows Czech venture investment of only €20.9m in 2024 ([Invest Europe CEE 2024](https://cvca.hr/wp-content/uploads/2025/12/Invest-Europe_CEE-Statistics_2024.pdf)). Smart Market, by contrast, reports CZK 14.3bn invested in Czech startups that year ([Forbes CZ](https://forbes.cz/investice-do-ceskych-startupu-vzrostly-o-174-procent-mews-a-rohlik-vedou-rekordy/)). For Slovakia, Dealroom's public page shows $336.3m of VC over the trailing 12 months, from an undated snapshot ([Dealroom Slovakia](https://dealroom.co/countries/slovakia/)). Invest Europe's figures are useful for cross-country ratios and fund counts, not as a census of deals.

### Slovakia's open financial statements are the region's best AUM source

Slovakia's standout asset is the RÚZ Open API ([RÚZ API](https://www.registeruz.sk/cruz-public/home/api)). It returns JSON under **CC0**, supports incremental sync through a changed-since parameter returning up to 10,000 records per call, exposes structured financial-statement line items, and updates throughout the day. Fund vehicles' balance sheets therefore give a machine-readable AUM proxy that few other jurisdictions offer. Some filings, however, are PDF-only or not public, and the latest year typically lags by 12–18 months.

The NBS financial-entities register lists 368 management companies (including foreign passported ones), **39 registered sub-threshold entities**, 92 Slovak funds and 1,049 foreign funds, but offers no visible export ([NBS register](https://subjekty.nbs.sk/?s=1059)).

Sub-threshold status applies up to €100m including leverage, or €500m for unleveraged funds with a five-year lock-up. Two rule changes will make Slovak VC and PE managers unusually easy to identify ([NBS registered managers](https://nbs.sk/en/financial-market-supervision1/supervision/collective-investment/registered-managers/)). From 1 June 2026, NBS can strike managers off the register by decision. By 30 December 2026, registered managers must add the designation "manager of sub-threshold risk capital funds" to their business name.

Two other Slovak registers are narrower. The RPO company register offers a free API that includes shareholders and statutory bodies ([Topograph](https://docs.topograph.co/essentials/slovakia)). RPVS publishes beneficial owners, but only for companies that contract with the public sector ([RPVS](https://www.justice.gov.sk/sluzby/register-partnerov-verejneho-sektora/); [Open Ownership](https://bods-data.openownership.org/source/slovakia)).

Slovakia's investor seed list is short and dominated by public money. SLOVCA has **12 full members**, a mix of PE houses (several based in Czechia or Poland), VC funds and public bodies, and only three of its 31 associates look like investors ([SLOVCA full members](https://www.slovca.sk/sk/clenstvo/riadni-clenovia.html); [SLOVCA associates](https://www.slovca.sk/sk/clenstvo/pridruzeni-clenovia.html)).

Public programmes run through Slovak Investment Holding (SIH). Its Venture to Future Fund (VFF) received €40.4m in 2019 and a further €15.3m in May 2024, writes tickets of €400k–€7m alongside private investors on equal terms, and invests until end-2026 ([SIH](https://www.sih.sk/en/aktuality/venture-to-future-fund-increases-capital-by-eur-15-3-million-to-support-slovak-startups)). ### Czechia has better discovery data and enough deal flow to validate against

The weakness is financials. Czech financial statements appear to be filed as documents in the register's collection of deeds rather than exposed through a structured API like RÚZ, though this research did not verify it. That makes AUM extraction more expensive.

Public LP programmes produce dated, citable evidence. ### Their moat is private inputs, not public facts

The incumbents gather data in three ways. Analyst- and survey-heavy vendors lead with direct contact: Preqin calls one-to-one conversations the backbone of its collection, supplemented by daily FOIA requests, filings and more than 450 analysts ([Preqin](https://www.preqin.com/data/data-coverage-map-view); [UMass Isenberg](https://www.isenberg.umass.edu/centers/center-for-international-securities-and-derivatives-markets/preqin-database)), and Wealth-X uses 400+ researchers and requires two independent sources ([Altrata](https://altrata.com/products/wealth-x)). Community-plus-ML vendors trade access for data: Crunchbase's 4,000+ venture-program members send monthly portfolio updates in exchange for data access ([Crunchbase Support](https://support.crunchbase.com/hc/en-us/articles/360009616013-Where-does-Crunchbase-get-their-data)), Dealroom cites 75, more than 90 and more than 100 government partners on different pages of its own site ([Dealroom vs PitchBook](https://dealroom.co/dealroom-vs-pitchbook); [Dealroom vs Crunchbase](https://dealroom.co/dealroom-vs-crunchbase); [Dealroom FAQ](https://dealroom.co/faq/)), and Signal lets investors list themselves. Beauhurst alone is registry-first, building on Companies House share-allotment filings.

A public-only product cannot replicate that moat: portfolio feeds traded for access, LP surveys, government partnerships and hundreds of analysts. Its inputs, however — Form D and ADV, Companies House, pension FOIA disclosures and press releases — are the same public ones the incumbents use, so parity on public facts is achievable.

### Verification is claimed everywhere and audited nowhere

Every vendor claims human verification, but none publishes record-level accuracy for investor fields. Crunchbase says its predictions anticipated 84% of funding events, yet the supporting figures on its own page are inconsistent and it discloses no method ([About Crunchbase Data](https://about.crunchbase.com/data)).

User reviews tell a different story. For PitchBook, 36 G2 reviews cite outdated information and 20 say its deal tags do not match reality ([G2](https://g2.com/products/pitchbook/reviews)), and 21% of TrustRadius reviewers raise timeliness ([TrustRadius](https://trustradius.com/products/pitchbook/reviews/all)); Crunchbase reviewers complain of missing (17) and outdated (15) information and rate contact accuracy 7.7/10 across 140 reviews ([G2 compare](https://www.g2.com/compare/crunchbase-vs-people-data-labs-vs-pitchbook)).

Investor type is a required field, and it is exactly where incumbents are weakest. The OECD found Crunchbase and Dealroom type tags so unharmonised that it surveyed member countries to validate 308 government VC entities across 38 economies. It also compiled corporate VC arms by hand and noted that both platforms lump angel groups, family offices and individuals together ([OECD](https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/publications/reports/2026/03/the-oecd-start-ups-database_6b7828eb/be8e5317-en.pdf)).

Ticket and AUM are thin for non-institutional investors. Preqin attaches AUM to fund managers rather than investors ([Preqin](https://www.preqin.com/data/private-equity)). Crunchbase investor profiles focus on deal counts ([Gigaboost](https://gigaboost.ai/blog/crunchbase-not-enough-investor-discovery/)). Founder tools carry self-reported check sizes ([Causo](https://hub.causo.ai/compare/openvc-vs-signal-nfx)).

## No benchmark measures investor fields, so plan for a ±10–15% floor

Every rigorous benchmark tests deals or funds, not investor profiles. Kaplan, Sensoy and Strömberg found that curated databases omitted about **15% of financing rounds** present in 143 actual VC contracts, though the amounts they did record were unbiased if noisy ([Kaplan, Sensoy & Strömberg](https://www.hhs.se/contentassets/dd8251e6b9f041bf83865e5a47e687c1/kaplansensoystrombergwp2002.pdf)). Kaplan and Lerner documented large inconsistencies between VentureXpert and VentureSource: across 449 firms present in both, VentureXpert held about 40% more rounds yet reported under 10% of investments as defunct when more than 20% were, and Preqin covered about 70% of committed capital for performance data in the 2000s while missing top funds without public-pension LPs ([Kaplan & Lerner](https://www.nber.org/system/files/chapters/c13495/c13495.pdf)). Retterath and Braun tested eight databases against 339 verified rounds at 108 mostly European companies and found VentureSource, PitchBook and Crunchbase the most complete and accurate, with larger rounds more likely to be reported and Crunchbase offering the best value ([SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=3706108)). Retterath's 2024 follow-up covered 554 institutional investors across eight vendors, but its per-database results could not be retrieved ([Retterath](https://x.com/AndreRetterath/status/1792941859691479306)), and Crunchbase's own claim of 85–95% round accuracy is self-published and predates 2024 ([Crunchbase blog](https://about.crunchbase.com/blog/crunchbase-goes-pound-for-pound-with-venturesource-insights-from-guillaume-goujon)).

The OECD's 2026 start-ups database, built on a June 2025 snapshot, is the best current cross-vendor comparison ([OECD](https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/publications/reports/2026/03/the-oecd-start-ups-database_6b7828eb/be8e5317-en.pdf)). Only **about 30% of entities** appear in both Crunchbase and Dealroom, and under 20% in Latin America; Crunchbase alone covers more than 40% of North American entities, while in Europe each source alone covers about 35%. Deals overlap more, at about 85% in North America, and the combined database finds 104% more European deal volume than national VC associations report, against only about 27% more in the US. Both platforms also register startups at an average age of about three years. Any single vendor therefore misses a third or more of entities outside its home region. Association statistics such as Invest Europe's EDC also under-count, which matches the Czech and Slovak gaps described earlier.

Ordinary published figures drift by 5–10%. NVCA's 2024 firm count reads 3,054 in its narrative and 3,152 in its table, and its 2023 count was revised from 3,417 to 3,199 between editions, about 7% ([NVCA 2026 Yearbook](https://nvca.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/NVCA-2026-Yearbook-4.9.26.pdf)). EBAN reports both 359 and 339 networks in the same document ([EBAN](https://www.eban.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/12/Stats-Compendium-2024.pdf)), and PitchBook's investor count varies by more than 50% depending on the citing source. Combined with the roughly 15% miss rate, this makes ±10–15% the realistic floor for coverage estimates.

Automated classification works well only for coarse labels. GPT-4o reached macro-F1 0.87 on multi-label cross-industry classification ([arXiv 2502.04095](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.04095)). On fine-grained NAICS codes, vanilla LLMs substantially underperform and sometimes invent codes that do not exist, while retrieval-augmented setups are competitive ([arXiv 2507.21112](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.21112)). A tuned T5 model reached 80.25% on sector inference ([arXiv 2306.03313](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2306.03313)). That suggests roughly 0.80–0.90 accuracy for coarse investor-type labels drawn from a closed set. Accuracy will be materially lower for fine-grained sector focus and for non-English websites, which one vendor says receive lower confidence ([Profound](https://www.profound.net/pages/resources/Digital_Industry_Classification_NAICS.pdf)). This applies directly to Czech and Slovak sources.

Industry codes are seeds only. A vendor citing ONS data says 11.5% of UK SIC codes do not match actual activity ([Veridion](https://veridion.com/insights/articles/why-legacy-codes-miss-niche-b2b-companies)). No precision/recall study exists for NACE 64.30 or SIC 64303 as investor identifiers.

Records decay on two clocks. At entity level, activity status falls about 15% a year in this cycle, and zombies make up about 18% of existing US VC firms. Contact data decays 20–30% a year by vendor estimates: 22.5% in HubSpot's simulation ([Airscale](https://airscale.io/blog/b2b-contact-data-decays-faster-than-you-think-here-is-the-evidence)), at least 23% in ZeroBounce data ([Landbase](https://landbase.com/blog/b2b-contact-data-accuracy-statistic)) and about 30% in figures traced to HBR and Gartner ([Cleanlist](https://cleanlist.ai/blog/2026-07-09-state-of-b2b-data-2026)).

Scraped pages also carry adversarial text. On 25 October 2026, Dealroom investor pages appeared in search results with a page title instructing LLMs to credit Dealroom as the data source ([Dealroom](https://app.dealroom.co/investors/kaplan_ventures)). Extraction pipelines must sanitise page text and must never let it trigger actions.

## Czechia should host the VC pilot, with Slovakia as a hand-built gold standard

Czechia is the better pilot on every dimension that decides whether a pilot can produce statistics.

| Dimension | Czechia | Slovakia |
|---|---|---|
| Estimated active VC managers | 30–50 | 8–15 |
| Locally based funds that raised capital, 2011–2025 | 30 | 8 |
| Cumulative fundraising, 2011–2025 | €1,958m | €252.8m |
| PE/VC investment received, 2025 | €275m | €7m |

Fund and investment figures come from [Invest Europe CEE 2025](https://www.investeurope.eu/media/z3pasguu/20260623_invest-europe_cee-report-2025.pdf).

Czechia's evidence base is also deeper. Its 75–100 rounds a year generate a steady stream of dated evidence. The EIF's 17 Czech funds, the recovery-plan fund-of-funds, NRI and JIC Ventures supply LP-grade proof. ČSÚ RES CSVs, justice.cz exports, ARES and JERRS support bulk discovery. With 30–50 records the pilot can produce meaningful statistics on precision and field completeness. With 5–15 it cannot.

The case for Slovakia deserves a hearing. Its VC universe is small enough to enumerate completely, which makes it the one place where true recall, not just precision, can be measured. Its CC0 RÚZ API is the best available test bed for extracting AUM from filed balance sheets. The new NBS naming rule will make investor-type identification unusually clean from December 2026.

Against that, a strict 24-month activity rule leaves only five to eight Slovak records, 2025 investment reached only five companies, and SIH's three new managers are not yet named. The best use of Slovakia is therefore as a gold standard: compile its full VC set by hand, use it to test the RÚZ AUM proxy and the NBS registered-manager flag, and add the three SIH managers once the award is published on TED.

The Czech pilot carries risks that should be cleared before it starts. The CVCA member list must be obtained directly, CzechCrunch's investor rankings are paywalled, and the noise ratio of the §15 list is unknown, so JERRS entity counts as at the pilot date should be pulled first. WS JERRS requires a certificate, AUM sits in filed documents rather than structured data, and extraction confidence on Czech-language sites will be lower.

Because no published benchmark exists, the pilot should record for every candidate whether a website, a regulatory filing and at least one dated deal were found, and whether AUM and ticket size are stated. It should also include a hand-checked accuracy sample for type, AUM and ticket.

## Conclusion

The binding constraint is not the number of sources but the definition of proof. Form D filings and LP disclosures prove that a fund raised money. Only deal announcements and registry share allotments prove that it deployed money. Ticket size is not disclosed anywhere, so the honest unit for that field is an observed ticket range backed by a count of cited deals. That is also more defensible than the self-reported check sizes in founder tools. The incumbents' advantage lies in private inputs that cannot be bought. Their weaknesses are ones a public-data product can exploit: unaudited "verified" labels, no per-field provenance, investor-type tags the OECD had to rebuild by hand, and licences that forbid reuse. A database that shows a source URL and date on every field, and publishes its own sample-based accuracy, competes on transparency rather than breadth. That positioning is open in a market consolidating into $30K–$50K suites.

Two further implications follow. First, the pilot itself will produce the first public benchmark of investor-attribute accuracy, because no academic or vendor study measures the accuracy of type, AUM or ticket fields. Second, the finished database will be small and institution-heavy by design: a few thousand verifiable institutional records per type globally and a few dozen in Czechia. Family-office and angel tiers should be explicitly labelled as lower-confidence. Coverage claims should carry ±10–15% bands, and activity should be re-verified every year, since roughly one in seven active investors drops out annually in the current cycle.
